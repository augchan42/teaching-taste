# Jiao Yanshou and Shao Yong

Research note on the intellectual lineage behind the *Jiaoshi Yilin* (焦氏易林) — background for *Teaching Taste to an Agent*.

---

Yes. You are seeing a **real intellectual family resemblance**. I would put Jiao Yanshou and Shao Yong on the same long **象數 *xiangshu* (“images and numbers”) trajectory**, although Shao Yong, living roughly **1,100 years later**, turns what in Jiao appears largely as a divinatory/calendrical technique into something approaching a universal mathematical cosmology.

The important caveat is that we know Jiao's thought mostly through later descriptions and especially through what his student **Jing Fang 京房** developed from it. The *Hanshu* says explicitly that Jiao's teaching divided the 64 hexagrams so that they successively “governed” particular days, and correlated them with **wind, rain, cold and warmth**, each providing observable signs and predictions. It also says he was interested in **pitch standards 鍾律**, another domain where number, cyclical periodicity, music and cosmic order intersect. ([Chinese Text Project][1])

Compare them:

|             | **Jiao Yanshou 焦延壽**                           | **Shao Yong 邵雍, 1011–1077 AD**                                          |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Yi          | Cosmic/divinatory operating system             | Cosmic/mathematical operating system                                    |
| Hexagrams   | Assigned through time                          | Systematically ordered as structures                                    |
| Yin/yang    | Waxing/waning qi produces observable phenomena | Waxing/waning generates the architecture of reality                     |
| Time        | Days, seasons, weather                         | Days → years → history → enormous cosmic cycles                         |
| Numbers     | Calendrical proportions, pitch-pipes           | Highly developed numerical/combinatorial schemes                        |
| Observation | Wind, rain, temperature, anomalies             | **觀物 guanwu**, observing things according to their underlying structure |
| Goal        | Prediction, especially 災變                      | Explain the structure and evolution of the entire cosmos                |

Shao's *Huangji Jingshi* 《皇極經世》 is much more ambitious. He uses numerical structures derived from the *Yi* to classify not just weather or omens but **the whole ordered world and human history**. Modern scholarship describes his project precisely as an attempt to represent reality as a coherent structure through numbers and *Yi* configurations. ([ScienceDirect][2])

### And there is a particularly intriguing connection

The later tradition itself noticed exactly what you noticed.

The Han/Jing tradition developed the famous **六日七分 — “six days and seven parts”** system: distributing the hexagrams through the year so that each governs a particular temporal interval. The *Lunheng* describes Jing Fang's scheme explicitly: 64 hexagrams distributed through the year; yin and yang within the hexagrams correspond to the rising and falling of qi and therefore cold and warmth. ([Chinese Text Project][3])

Then, centuries later, Zhu Xi records this:

> 京房卦氣用六日七分。季通云：「康節亦用六日七分。」但不見康節說處。

Roughly:

> **“Jing Fang's hexagram-qi uses six days and seven parts. [Cai] Jitong says Kangjie [Shao Yong] also used six days and seven parts. But I have not seen where Kangjie himself says this.”**

([Chinese Text Project][4])

That's almost exactly the historical question you're asking.

Zhu Xi himself was being cautious: **there seems to be continuity, but he couldn't demonstrate the textual transmission.**

Even more strikingly, a later discussion of Jiao's system explicitly compares it with Shao's and says that although **their hexagram arrangements are different**, both treat:

**復 Fu → 乾 Qian** as the development of **yang**

and

**姤 Gou → 坤 Kun** as the development of **yin**. ([Chinese Text Project][5])

That's not a superficial similarity. It is the same fundamental picture:

> **Reality is a cyclic process of quantifiable yin-yang phase change, and the hexagrams are not merely symbols describing that process — they constitute a formal model of it.**

And I think that's the key to understanding both men.

### Where Shao makes the giant leap

Jiao's model seems essentially **predictive**:

**What phase of qi are we in? → what phenomena should manifest? → what might happen?**

Shao asks a more radical question:

**What if that same structure generates everything?**

So the day becomes analogous to the year; the year analogous to historical epochs; historical epochs analogous to the lifespan of Heaven and Earth. Shao takes recursive temporal correspondence to immense scales—the famous **元 yuan → 會 hui → 運 yun → 世 shi** structure and a grand cycle of 129,600 years.

It's almost a kind of **scale invariance**:

> the same pattern is instantiated at different temporal resolutions.

That strikes me as the biggest difference.

Jiao/Jing are doing something close to **cosmic forecasting**.

Shao is trying to construct a **generative model of the universe**.

### So was Shao Yong actually descended intellectually from Jiao?

**Broadly, yes; demonstrably person-to-person, no.**

Shao's traditional immediate lineage runs through **Li Zhicai 李之才 → Mu Xiu 穆修 → Zhong Fang 种放 → Chen Tuan 陳摶**, although even the precise history of that transmission is controversial. Later sources explicitly present Shao as inheriting this *xiantian* diagram tradition. ([Chinese Text Project][6])

But underneath that Song tradition lies a much older body of **Han image-number, hexagram-qi, calendrical and numerical Yi scholarship**, and **Jiao Yanshou → Jing Fang** is one of its most important early branches.

So I'd draw the intellectual history less as:

**Jiao → ... → Shao**

and more as:

**Jiao Yanshou → Jing Fang → Han 卦氣 / 象數 tradition**
　　　　　　　　　↓
　　　　　centuries of transformation
　　　　　　　　　↓
**Chen Tuan tradition → Li Zhicai → Shao Yong**
　　　　　　　　　↓
　　　　**Huangji Jingshi**

Shao Yong therefore looks to me like a **massive Song-dynasty re-generalization of a style of thinking already very visible in Jiao Yanshou**: hexagrams as states of a cyclic system, qi as the changing variable, numbers as the grammar, and observable reality as the output.

And this also makes the *Jiaoshi Yilin* authorship issue even more interesting. **The 4,096 poetic judgments are not obviously the sort of thing the historical Jiao is described as being obsessed with.** The historical Jiao sounds much more like **Shao Yong's distant ancestor intellectually: calendars, cycles, weather, music, numbers and phase transitions.**
